# Indoor Herb Gardening

Fresh herbs change the way a kitchen feels and the way food tastes. A small pot of basil on the windowsill or a row of mint by the sink puts flavour within arm's reach all year round, even when the weather outside is cold or wet. Indoor herb gardening needs no big garden, no special tools and very little space — a sunny spot, a few pots and a little regular care are enough to keep fresh leaves growing for months.

## Choosing Herbs for Indoors

Start with herbs that forgive beginners. Basil grows fast and loves warmth and sun, mint is almost impossible to kill but needs its own pot because it spreads, and parsley and chives cope well with less light. Rosemary and thyme prefer drier soil and plenty of sun, so they suit a bright south-facing window. Grow what you actually cook with: if you make pasta and salads, basil and parsley earn their place first, and if you drink a lot of tea, mint is the obvious choice. Buy small plants from a garden centre rather than seeds for your first try — you get usable leaves within days instead of weeks.

## Light, Water and Soil

Most herbs need at least six hours of light a day, so a sunny windowsill is the best home for them. If your windows face north or the winter days are short, a small grow light a few inches above the plants makes a real difference. Water when the top inch of soil feels dry, and always let extra water drain away — herbs hate sitting in soggy soil, and wet roots rot quickly. Use ordinary potting mix with a little perlite or coarse sand mixed in so it drains freely, and feed lightly once a month with a weak liquid fertiliser during the growing season.

## Pots, Containers and Drainage

The pot matters as much as the plant. Every container needs drainage holes, because water that cannot escape will drown the roots within days. Terracotta pots are a good choice for herbs: they breathe, they dry out between waterings, and their weight keeps top-heavy plants steady. Plastic pots hold moisture longer, which suits basil and mint but can be too wet for rosemary and thyme. A saucer under each pot catches drips, but empty it after watering so the plant is never left standing in water. When a plant outgrows its pot, move it up one size only — a pot that is too large holds wet soil around small roots and invites rot. A layer of small stones or broken crockery at the bottom of the pot helps water drain freely, and a light mulch of fine gravel on the surface keeps the soil from drying out too fast on a sunny sill.

## Harvesting and Keeping Plants Healthy

Pick leaves little and often, and always take from the top of the plant: pinching the growing tips encourages the plant to branch out and grow bushier instead of tall and leggy. Never strip more than a third of a plant at once, and let it recover before the next harvest. Turn pots every few days so all sides get light, and snip off any yellow leaves promptly. Watch for tiny flies around the soil — they usually mean the soil is too wet, so let it dry out more between waterings. A healthy indoor herb plant can keep producing for a year or more with this simple routine.

## Feeding, Pruning and Seasonal Care

Herbs in pots use up the food in their soil faster than plants in the ground, so a little feeding goes a long way. A weak liquid fertiliser once a month in spring and summer is enough; too much food makes soft, flavourless growth. Pruning is just as important as feeding. Cut back woody herbs like rosemary and thyme in spring to keep them compact, and pinch out the flower buds on basil as soon as they appear — once basil flowers, its leaves turn bitter. In winter, move pots away from cold glass at night and cut back on watering, because plants grow slowly in short days and wet soil in cold weather is the fastest way to lose them. If a plant looks tired after a year, take cuttings from its healthiest stems and start again: fresh plants are often stronger than old, woody ones.

## Common Problems and Easy Fixes

Most indoor herb problems have simple causes. Yellow leaves usually mean too much water or too little light, so check the soil before watering again and move the pot to a brighter spot. Leggy stems with few leaves mean the plant is stretching for light — cut it back by half and move it closer to the window. White mould on the soil surface is harmless but a sign of poor air flow; scrape it off and open a window nearby for an hour a day. Aphids sometimes appear on soft new growth; a strong spray of water or a wipe with soapy water removes them without chemicals. If a plant suddenly drops its leaves, it has usually been shocked by a cold draught or a missed watering — trim the dead stems, water lightly, and give it a week in a warm, bright spot before deciding it is lost.

## Using Your Herbs in the Kitchen

The real reward of an indoor herb garden is in the cooking. Tear basil into pasta at the last minute so its scent stays fresh, and chop parsley into salads and soups just before serving. Mint lifts tea, fruit salads and summer drinks, while rosemary and thyme stand up to roasting and slow cooking — add them early so their flavour soaks into the dish. Dry any surplus by hanging small bunches upside down in a warm, airy room, then crumble the leaves into jars. Fresh herbs also freeze well: chop them, pack them into ice-cube trays with a little water or oil, and drop a cube straight into the pan. Once you have fresh leaves a few steps from the stove, shop-bought dried herbs start to feel like a poor substitute.

An indoor herb garden is one of the easiest and most rewarding ways to start growing your own food. Start with two or three pots, learn what each plant likes, and add more as your confidence grows.
